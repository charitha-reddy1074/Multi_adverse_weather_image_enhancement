Frequency-Oriented Efficient Transformer for
All-in-One Weather-Degraded Image Restoration

Abstract— Adverse weather conditions, such as rain, raindrop,
snow and haze, consistently degrade images in an unpre-
dictable manner, thereby rendering existing task-specific and
task-aligned methods inadequate in addressing this formidable
problem. To this end, we investigate the application of
Transformer in image restoration and introduce an efficient
frequency-oriented method called AIRFormer, which is designed
to restore weather-degraded images comprehensively and holis-
tically. Specifically, we identify that the initial self-attention
mechanism exhibits distinctive properties akin to a low-pass filter.
Therefore, we construct a frequency-guided Transformer encoder
by incorporating wavelet-based prior information to guide
the extraction of image features. Additionally, considering the
non-specific frequency characteristics of self-attention in the later
stages, we develop a frequency-refined Transformer decoder that
incorporates learnable task-specific queries across spatial dimen-
sions, channel dimensions, and wavelet domains. To facilitate the
training of our proposed method, we curate a comprehensive
benchmark dataset named AIR40K that, encompasses a wide
range of challenging scenarios. Extensive experimental evalua-
tions demonstrate the superiority of our AIRFormer over both
task-aligned and all-in-one methods across 15 publicly available
datasets. Notably, AIRFormer achieves the best trade-off between
the inference time and quality of reconstructed image, comparing
with existing methods such as TransWeather and Restormer. The
source code, dataset and pre-trained models will be available at
https://github.com/chdwyb/AIRFormer.
Index Terms— Computer vision, image restoration, adverse
weather removal, frequency-oriented transformer.

However, most Transformer-based methods [15], [16], [17],

based on spatial features, without adequately considering the
knowledge of frequency variation. In this paper, we investi-
gate the general principle of Transformer in terms of image
restoration [15], [17] and aim to unify multiple tasks related to
adverse weather removal by incorporating frequency changes.
We discover that early self-attention exhibits low-pass-like
characteristics, while late SA does not function as a prominent
filtering mechanism, as depicted in Figure 2. Although differ-
ent weather degradations may have different frequency band,
however their frequency variations of features in the u-shaped
architectures are similar, which facilitates our establishment of
all-in-one method. Our findings suggest that image restoration
networks tend to capture low-frequency information during the
early stages and effectively eliminate degradations present in
the high-frequency components. Subsequently, the reconstruc-
tion network gradually restores high-frequency information to
preserve image details while removing degradations. To some
extent, this phenomenon explains the use of “pooling” oper-
ations and the performance enhancement achieved by SA in
the later stages of PoolFormer [24]. With a natural division
line between the early and late stages in the U-net architec-
ture [25], we employ a U-shaped PoolFormer as our baseline
encoder. Degradations, such as rain streaks [26] and snow
particles [27], are often small in scale, leading to difficul-
ties when attending to larger image patches. This limitation
hampers the generalization performance without additional
supplementary information. To address this issue, we propose
a supplementary prior (SP) module that utilizes depth-wise
wavelet convolution to fuse features and selectively filter
high-frequency components while preserving low-frequency
components. The SP module, in conjunction with frequency
prior information, facilitates additive guidance for feature
learning. Additionally, it enables the Transformer encoder to
focus on features within patches after the patch embedding
process. Although our method is frequency-oriented, which
may not be necessary in the frequency domain for degraded
images, it can be equivalently implemented using spatial
operations.
Most Transformer-based methods typically define tokens
as pixels or patches in the spatial dimension. However,
in regard to the unspecific frequency changes in the late
stage, it becomes crucial to equally focus on both low and
high frequencies to restore images with rich details and
accurate regions. By visualizing the feature maps, we find that
the spectra of different channels are not exactly the same.
Fcanet [28] demonstrates that channel attention primarily
enhances low frequencies, indicating that self-attention in
the channel dimension emphasizes low-frequency information,
while self-attention in the spatial dimension refines frequency
at the resolution level. Furthermore, a feature map encom-
passes both low and high frequencies, implying that more
detailed attention is advantageous for image reconstruction
and detail preservation. Inspired by [29], we employ a discrete
wavelet transformer (DWT) to refine features into frequency
components, enhancing feature representation in the frequency
domain. In addition, vision Transformer always represents Q,
K and V vectors are typically derived from the same input
features, resulting in strong dependencies between attention
and input features. This characteristic limits the generalization
and transferability of the model when dealing with multiple
degradations. According to the research in [30] and [22],
we introduce special task-biased queries into the frequency
refinement decoder, where the Q performs as prior knowledge
related to the consistency of different weather conditions.
Meanwhile, most methods [14], [17], [21], [22] primarily
focus on the error in the spatial domain while disregarding
the error in the frequency domain. This omission hinders the
effective enforcement of frequency consistency between the
restored and degradation-free images. To address this, we pro-
pose a multi-domain consistency loss function to enhance the
quality of restored images.

II. R ELATED WORK
A. Task-specific Image Restoration
Early studies establish specialized models for degraded
images to characterize the composition of degradation and
corresponding ground truth as shown in Figure 3 (a). An image
degraded by rain streaks is often modeled as [26], [31], [32]
I = B + Rstreak , (1)
where I is the rainy image, B is the rain-free image, and
Rstreak is the rain streaks layer. In the following formu-
las, we define I as the contaminated image and B as
the degradation-free. For instance, DID-MDN [33] estimates
rain-density and removes rain streaks using a multi-stream
densely connected network. However, it fails to handle the
degradation like haze. Liu et al. [27] model the snowy image
as
I = M ⊙ S + B ⊙ (1 − S), (2)
where M is a mask indicating snow, ⊙ denotes element-wise
multiplication, S is the snow layer. According to [12], raindrop
degenerates image in the way of
I = (1 − D) ⊙ B + Rdrop , (3)
where D is a binary mask and Rdrop is the raindrop layer. The
physical model of an image occluded by fog [34], [35] is
I = T ⊙ B + (1 − T ) ⊙ A, (4)
where T is the transmission map produced by scattering
effect, A is the scene atmospheric light value. However,
the composition and distribution of pollution in reality are
different, yet specific modeling limits the solution space and
leads to inability to adapt to the diversity [22], [27], [36], [37],

CNN and Transformer are introduced to improve the net-
work generalization ability to single-degradation, and achieve
great successes over traditional models in single-degradation
image restoration tasks, like deraining [26], [33], [37], [38],
[39], [40], desnowing [10], [27], [31], [41], [42], raindrop

different degradations has gradually received attention.
B. Task-Aligned Image Restoration
Although there are inconsistencies between different degra-
dation principles, the powerful representational capability of
deep learning leads to task-aligned single-degradation image
restoration methods [14], [16], [17], [54], [55], which can be
expressed as
B = fθρ (Iρ), (6)
where fθρ denotes the same solution with parameters θρ
corresponding to the degraded image Iρ. Among these meth-
ods as shown in Figure 3 (b), [56] presents a multi-level
wavelet CNN for enlarging receptive field with better trade-off
between efficiency and effectiveness. MIRNet [57] establishes
a new feature extraction model, which preserves the original
high-resolution features while maintaining accurate spatial
details. Later, a style of residual connection is presented,
dubbed “dual residual connection” [58], aiming to exploit the
potential of paired operations. CLEARER [59] proposes a
multi-resolution searching space to find suitable architectures
and finally achieve promising performance in denoising and
deraining. Recently, MPRNet [14] designs a multi-stage net-
work that produces context-rich and spatially accurate output
to achieve progressive restoration, and HINet [60] similarly
achieves competitive performance with less computation and
time-consumption based on half instance normalization. Along
with the development of vision Transformer, SwinIR [15],
Restormer [17] and Uformer [16] achieve quite good perfor-
mance on several image restoration tasks. At the near present,
MAXIM [18] conducts a general image processing framework
based on multi-axis MLP. Although task-aligned methods
present effective generalization and superior performance, the
separate training and long-term inference are the stumbling
blocks in practical application.
C. All-in-One Image Restoration
Previous methods either establish corresponding models for
specific task [12], [26], [27], [48], or learn unified solutions
but trained separately [14], [16], [17], [18], without exploring
the commonality among different degradations to achieve AIR.
There are two formulas for AIR, as shown in Figure 3 (c)
B = tρ,θ ρ ( fθ (gρ,θ ρ (Iρ))), (7)
where tρ,θ ρ and gρ,θ ρ are the processing tail and head for Iρ,
fθ are the parameters that remains constant after leaning for
all degradations. Please note that tρ is not always considered.
For example, [19] proposes an AIR method, which contains
different acceptance heads for several bad weather conditions.
In contrast, IPT [20] develops a pre-trained image processing
framework with specific tails for each type of pollution. The
other formula is
B = fθ (Iρ), (8)
where fθ is self-same for all weather-degraded image restora-
tion tasks as shown in Figure 3 (d). In related research, [61]
proposes a decoupled learning framework to adapt several
image restoration tasks, which is similar to late [21], [62].
Besides, the method named TransWeather [22] is designed for
multi-degraded image restoration with only one single encoder
and decoder. Therefore, the all-in-one methods formulated
as (8) perform as the promising and effective approaches to
eliminate multiple weather degradations unitedly.
D. Vision Transformer
Transformer has been widely adopted in up-/low-stream
computer vision tasks [16], [17], [30], [63], [64], [65],
and shown considerable competitiveness. Current works on
Transformer believe that spatial SA can effectively learn
long-range dependencies and adapt to the input, therefore
focus on improving the token mixers, like window parti-
tioning [64], [66], relative position coding [67], convolution
combining [68], [69], [70] and formula optimizing [71], [72].
Beyond above, some other works [73], [74], [75] simply
replace SA with MLP-like modules and still achieve compara-
ble effects, which questions the dominance of attention-based
architectures and inspires the work on what plays the core
role in Transformer. For example, PoolFormer [24] applies
a simple operator named “pooling”, which outperforms the
mainstream vision Transformer-based methods [64], [74], [76],
[77]. However, few works explore and clarify the frequency
characteristics of Transformer in image restoration.
III. P ROPOSED AIRF ORMER
Our main purpose is to explore the feature frequency
varying tendency of vision Transformer in image restoration,
thus accordingly design a frequency-oriented efficient Trans-
former to restore the degraded image under adverse weather
conditions. The overall pipeline of our proposed AIRFormer
is shown in Figure 4. Notably, although our AIRFormer
is frequency-oriented, which processes the weather-degraded
image in spatial domain equivalently instead of introducing
extra frequency transformation into architectural establish-
ment. The main components in our proposed AIRFormer are
illustrated specifically in the following sections.

A. Frequency-Guided Transformer Encoder
Initially, we explore the frequency characteristics of SA
and MLP in the pretrained image restoration networks,
SwinIR [15] and Restormer [17]. Surprisingly, we find that
early SA is low-pass-like while late SA does not work as
an obvious filter function. The approximations of early SA
and “pooling” in frequency properties further support the
appropriate adoption of PoolFormer [24] in our baseline of
Transformer encoder. However, adverse weather conditions
present both approximations and differences, which causes
unguided learning to exacerbate the training difficulty. Existing
work rarely embeds the frequency information, while a few
turn it into a backbone component instead of a bootstrap
module. For example, Fnet [82] replaces self-attention with
fast Fourier transform. However, our experiments suggest that
this kind of approach leads to worse performance than self-
attention-based Transformer in image restoration.
The research in [29] reveals that discrete wavelet transform
(DWT) [83] improves the representation ability of vision
Transformer. We attribute it to the fact that, DWT down-
samples the two-dimensional image into wavelet sub-bands
and achieves a fine separation of high/low frequencies. Since
the frequency changes significantly in image restoration, the
separation and enhancement of frequencies are beneficial. The
2-dimensional DWT is defined as

B. Frequency-Refined Transformer Decoder
The non-specific filtering characteristics and a fact [24] that
the classification accuracy is significantly improved by replac-
ing “ pooling” with SA in later learning period, suggesting it
is worse to apply the same token mixer in our Transformer
decoder. Therefore, based on the frequency variation in image
restoration, we rethink feature decoder architecture in terms
of refining frequency.
Most Transformer-based image restoration methods [16],
[17] define token as pixel or patch, aggregating context in spa-
tial dimension. However, there are differences in the spectrum
regarding the same set of features. In [28], the channel atten-
tion is verified to be the lowest-frequency component of dis-
crete cosine transformer (DCT). Obviously, channel-dimension
self-attention is a simple proportional transformation in fre-
quency domain from different channels. Based on this,
we refine feature frequencies and transfer attention to channel
dimension for realizing weighted frequencies transformation.
Therefore, channel attention in either CNN or Transformer is
a representation in low-frequency level feature. Another fact
is that spatial self-attention is more powerful than channel
attention in modeling rich frequency dependencies, thereby we
first adopt these two-dimension SA to realize frequency-wise
refinement. However, Zheng et al. [84] recently demonstrates
that self-attention is less powerful than convolution to capture
high-frequency dependencies, which inspires us that high/low
frequency separation may improve this disadvantage. Further-
more, we consider that simply calculating SA in both space
and channel dimension misses the contribution discrepancy
at different frequencies levels in single feature plain. Based
on this, we adopt DWT to further refine feature frequencies
and represent self-attention mapping in wavelet domain. The
computation of SA in spatial dimension, channel dimension
and wavelet domain focuses on frequency changes from a
more holistic and detailed perspective, thereby facilitates the
reconstruction of multi-weather degraded image. Although
these main architectures have existed, no research ever unified
them in terms of frequency refinement to our best knowledge.
In addition, Transformer always represents self-attention
from pre-positioned feature maps, and exhibits over-strong
dependencies between self-attention and input features, result-
ing in the limitations of generalization and transferability.
According to [30] and [22], we construct task-biased queries
separately for frequency-refined decoder. Different with [22],
it constrains the attention by both a set of learnable vectors
and input features. We illustrate the task-queried attention
blocks in Figure 5, where Q is not all from input features as
usual, but vectors partially representing task knowledge based
on the particularity of weather-degraded image restoration.
In this approach, the features decoded by our decoder not
only employ sufficiently frequency information, but also are
directed by the all-in-one task-biased queries.

B. Implementation Details
Our proposed AIRFormer is trained on 256 × 256 random-
cropped patches from our collected AIR40K dataset.
We exploit the AdamW optimizer (β 1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999,
weight decay 10 -4) with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−4,
which are steadily decreased to 1 × 10−7 by the cosine
annealing decay [85]. Meanwhile, random horizontal and
vertical flips, 90 ◦ rotation and MixUp [86] with probability
0.5 perform the data augmentation. When testing, we pad the
input images to a multiplier of 128 × 128 using symmetric
padding on both sides. After inference, we crop the padded
images back to original size, thus our AIRFormer adapts to
all sizes of the degraded images. All the experiments are
performed on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090.

D. Limitations
We exquisitely design the AIRFormer through exploring
the tendency of features frequency changing in the well-tuned
SwinIR [15] and Restormer [17], while the specific reason of
frequency changing is not discussed in detail. Moreover, our
work focuses only on the four common weather conditions
without considering more weather type degradations, like dust,
dark light, etc.
VI. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we explore Transformer in feature frequencies
and find that the early SA performs low-pass-like while the
later is unspecific. Our discoveries verify the improvement
of PoolFormer with “pooling” and attention. Furthermore,
we build a hierarchical frequency-guided encoder to extract
and generate features from degraded images, which is supple-
mented with the frequency of original patches. Meanwhile,
we refine the feature frequencies and represent attention
queried by the task-biased vectors from channel dimension,
spatial dimension and wavelet domain. In addition, we also
formulate a multi-domain loss function to keep the consistency
of restored image in spatial and frequency domains. Extensive
experiments on both synthetic and real-world multi-degraded
images shows that our AIRformer achieves real-time perfor-
mance while outperforms the current state-of-the-art methods.
